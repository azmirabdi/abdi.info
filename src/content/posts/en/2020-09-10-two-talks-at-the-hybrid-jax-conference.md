Three very unusual JAX [[1]](https://jax.de/mainz/) days. A conference that is usually thick with people who care about IT, where check-in and the cloakroom can take up to an hour. That such an event could take place during the pandemic was unusual too. The organiser, Software & Support Media GmbH [2], put together a bundle of measures to make it possible. The whole conference was built as a hybrid: you could attend in the room or online. A professional video-streaming crew, the sort that usually (there is that “usually” again) accompanies large concerts, transmitted every session. The sessions ran in parallel in eight rooms, spread across three locations at the same time. Through the online platform Swapcard [3], online participants followed the sessions as a stream and could join the discussion in chat. People in the room stayed with the same group, in the same room, for a morning or an afternoon. Masks, distance, and hygiene, as was usual then, were required when moving through the building.

I was able to give two talks, each with a colleague. The first was about integration tests in a complex microservice environment [[4]](https://jax.de/software-architecture/architektur-der-integrationstests-in-der-aera-der-microservices/). The second was about cross-cutting business processes, again in a complex microservice environment [[5]](https://jax.de/microservices/microservices-und-bpm-liebe-it-denkst-du-noch-an-das-geschaeft/). I have been occupied for a while with the microservice paradigm as an IT architecture, and with the agile way of working that comes with it. That has consequences for every part of IT, including cross-cutting business processes and the integration tests, or *end-to-end* tests, that belong to them. The talks offer approaches for covering these areas in an agile way as well.

It was new for me, as a speaker, to keep an eye not only on the people in the room but also on the people signed in through the online platform, and on the online discussion that went with them. Other speakers were remote themselves and could see only the online platform directly. On-site moderators supported them from the rooms, and over Zoom they could pick up a few impressions of the room.

Overall, this hybrid conference ran, for a first time, with surprisingly few technical mishaps. Credit to the organiser for that. Hybrid conferences, and hybrid workshops between people in the room and people remote, are a solution for the present, and they are needed. All of us, and the technology and tools, still have to get better at this kind of collaboration. That will happen with time. I am curious how we will actually handle it in a few years.

Thanks for your time. Warm regards,
